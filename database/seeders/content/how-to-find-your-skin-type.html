You can find your skin type at home with a simple bare-face test: wash your face with a gentle cleanser, pat it dry, apply nothing, and wait 30 minutes. Then check how your skin looks and feels. Shiny all over means oily, tight or flaky means dry, shine only on the forehead and nose means combination, and stinging or redness points to sensitive skin.

Source: The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recognizes five skin types: normal, dry, oily, combination, and sensitive. Test methods are drawn from Cleveland Clinic and Healthline skin-type guides. This article is general information, not medical advice.

What Are the Different Types of Facial Skin?

Dermatology groups facial skin into five types: normal, dry, oily, combination, and sensitive. Skin type mostly depends on how much sebum (natural skin oil) your skin produces.

Sensitive skin is the exception. It is not about oil level, so you can have sensitive skin that is also dry, oily, or combination.

How Do You Do the Bare-Face Test at Home?

This is the easiest and cheapest skin type test, and it needs no products.

  1. Wash your face with a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser and lukewarm water.
  2. Pat dry with a clean towel. Do not rub.
  3. Apply nothing. No toner, serum, moisturizer, or sunscreen.
  4. Wait 30 minutes in a normal room, without touching your face.
  5. Look in natural light and notice shine, tightness, and flakiness on your forehead, nose, cheeks, and chin.

How Do You Do the Tissue Paper (Blotting) Test?

  1. Wash your face and dry it, as above.
  2. Wait about 30 minutes.
  3. Gently press a blotting paper or clean tissue on your forehead, nose, cheeks, and chin.
  4. Hold it up to the light.

Reading the result: Cleveland Clinic advises that if your skin still feels oily after about 30 minutes, you might have oily skin. If it still feels dry, you might have dry skin. If some areas are oily and others dry, you may have combination skin. A lot of oil on the paper from all areas suggests oily skin, oil only from the T-zone suggests combination, and little to no oil suggests normal or dry skin.

Plain tissue paper can work, but dedicated blotting sheets show oil more clearly.

How Do You Identify Oily Skin?

Signs of oily skin:

  • Shine across the whole face within a few hours of washing
  • Visibly larger pores
  • Makeup that slips or fades quickly
  • More frequent pimples or blackheads

Oily skin is more likely to be acne-prone, but acne-prone is a tendency, not a skin type by itself.

How Do You Identify Dry Skin?

Signs of dry skin:

  • A tight feeling, especially after washing
  • Rough texture, flaking, or dry patches
  • Itchiness
  • Little to no shine, even by evening

Dry skin lacks oil. Dehydrated skin lacks water and can happen to any skin type, so a tight feeling alone does not always mean dry skin.

How Do You Identify Combination Skin?

Signs of combination skin:

  • Oily T-zone (forehead, nose, chin)
  • Normal or dry cheeks
  • Different areas needing different products

Some skincare sources describe combination skin as one of the most common types, and it can swing with the season. It may feel oilier in warm, humid weather and drier in winter, and it can shift with hormones and stress.

How Do You Identify Sensitive Skin?

Signs of sensitive skin:

  • Redness or flushing
  • Stinging or burning when you apply products
  • Itching or irritation
  • Reactions to fragrance, strong cleansers, or new products

Sensitivity is a condition layered on top of a skin type. Healthline and dermatology guides note that dry skin is more often sensitive, but oily skin can be sensitive too.

If you have severe burning, swelling, rashes, or ongoing eczema-like patches, see a dermatologist instead of self-testing.

What Mistakes Do People Make When Finding Their Skin Type?

  1. Testing after using products. Moisturizer and serums change the result.
  2. Testing when skin is irritated. Wait until it settles.
  3. Judging by one hour. Wait the full 30 minutes.
  4. Confusing dehydration with dry skin. Oily skin can still feel tight if it lacks water.
  5. Treating sensitivity as a skin type. It can occur with any type.
  6. Using harsh cleansers. Over-washing can make skin oilier or more irritated.
  7. Testing only once. Repeat in different seasons.

Can Your Skin Type Change Over Time?

Yes. Healthline notes that skin can change over time, with the seasons, and for other reasons. Common triggers include weather and climate changes, hormonal shifts, stress, age, and moving to a new place. Retest every few months or when your skin starts behaving differently.

How Do You Choose Skincare Products for Your Skin Type?

Start simple: cleanser, moisturizer, and sunscreen (SPF 30 or higher, daily).

  • Oily: lightweight, oil-free (non-comedogenic) gel or lotion textures
  • Dry: richer creams; look for hydrating ingredients such as glycerin or hyaluronic acid
  • Combination: a gentle cleanser and a light moisturizer, with extra moisture on dry areas only
  • Sensitive: fragrance-free, simple formulas; introduce one new product at a time

Patch-test any new product on a small area for a few days first.

What Is a Simple Routine for Each Skin Type?

Oily skin: Gentle cleanser (morning and night), light moisturizer, sunscreen. Do not skip moisturizer, because dry, stressed skin can produce more oil.

Dry skin: Cream or hydrating cleanser, rich moisturizer applied on damp skin, sunscreen. Avoid hot showers on your face.

Combination skin: Gentle cleanser, light moisturizer all over, a richer layer on dry cheeks if needed, sunscreen.

Sensitive skin: Minimal routine of gentle cleanser, fragrance-free moisturizer, mineral sunscreen if regular sunscreen stings.
